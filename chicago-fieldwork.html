Field research & desk research · Durham · 2025

Stadium redevelopment in Chicago

I arrived in Chicago with a critical reading of stadium redevelopment. Comparing places and speaking with workers made that argument more specific: ownership, control and everyday use mattered as much as the promotional narrative.

Site observation / Informal interviewing / Evidence synthesis / Reflective judgement

A question taken into the field

My earlier essay examined public-benefit claims around the Bears’ stadium proposals. During a university field trip, I compared that desk research with observations at Soldier Field and Wrigleyville, alongside other sites across the city. The field notebook became a record of where an initial interpretation held up and where it needed to change.

One change in interpretation

Before the conversation

An apparently missed opportunity

At Soldier Field, I noticed limited surrounding commercial activity. My initial interpretation was that the area might be underused or neglected.

A different piece of evidence

Who could decide what happened there?

Two fan-shop staff described the distinction between the team and the stadium’s public ownership. Their account made me reconsider how control over the site could shape what I had observed.

Revised interpretation

Governance became part of the explanation

I reframed the question around ownership and decision-making, rather than inferring neglect from a quiet setting. That was a more grounded hypothesis, not a definitive explanation established by two conversations.

A useful contrast at Wrigleyville

Wrigleyville presented a different scene: visible branding, shops and activity extending beyond the stadium. Conversations with workers at an official shop and a private store added accounts of neighbourhood change. I used the contrast to examine how a venue’s commercial presence reaches into everyday urban space, while avoiding the assumption that commercial intensity itself proves community benefit.

What I did with conflicting evidence

The earlier literature helped me question public-benefit claims and public-private incentives. Fieldwork complicated that frame: a quiet setting was not enough to infer neglect, just as an active commercial district was not enough to establish community benefit. I used the contrast to develop more precise questions about who controlled each place and how that control shaped its use.

Scope of the work

Situated observations and informal conversations from a university field trip, interpreted alongside earlier desk research. The accounts support a revised explanation to investigate, rather than a representative evaluation of redevelopment outcomes.

Two pieces of work, one case +

Redevelopment as Rhetoric, February 2025, supplied the initial argument about public-benefit narratives and private gains. Curated Cities, Contested Spaces, May 2025, records the later fieldwork, particularly the Soldier Field and Wrigleyville sections on pages 12–14. The field accounts are paraphrased here to show how the interpretation developed.
