Job access and the Lelylijn

Modelling access to jobs along a proposed Dutch rail corridor.

I built a transport-accessibility analysis of the proposed Lelylijn, combining timetables, street networks and neighbourhood data. I then tested how the assessment changed with travel-time limits, evaluation boundaries and different definitions of fairness.

The model points to concentrated improvements around the corridor, with much weaker evidence of broad regional rebalancing.

Interpreting the reach

The distribution of gains

The primary evaluation population accounts for 9.8% of residents in the broad study area, but captures 95.3% of its population-weighted accessibility gain at 90 minutes.

Expanding the evaluation to wider regions makes the measured reduction in inequality much smaller. The boundary is part of the analytical question.

How was this population defined? +

Neighbourhoods with an existing walking-and-transit journey of up to 45 minutes to a proposed service point. This was defined before observing the scenario’s gains. That 45-minute rule defines the population; it is separate from the job-accessibility budget on the map.

Different measures of fairness

Gains favour residents starting with poorer access, but do not consistently favour the lowest-income neighbourhoods. Improving the geography of opportunity is only one dimension of fairness.

The benchmark matters too. Fewer residents fall below a fixed level of job access, while the share whose relative access falls short of socioeconomic need rises slightly.

One proposal, different questions

Change in inequality

All comparisons use 90-minute job access. Measures answer different questions and use different scales; they are not an overall score of the project’s merit.

Behind the analysis

Methods and sources

The approach +

I combined transport timetables, street networks and neighbourhood data, estimated employment locations and constructed a modelled rail service. I used r5r to compare walking-and-transit access on both networks, then tested different travel-time budgets, population boundaries and distributional measures.

Employment estimates were checked against independent municipal job totals. The study also examined socioeconomic rankings and alternative ways of measuring low accessibility.

The limits +

This isolates a direct network effect on potential access. It does not predict job creation, relocation, individual travel choices or the railway’s economic return. Job counts are estimated, neighbourhood characteristics are averages, and service assumptions remain those of a proposed network.

Reachable jobs are not all attainable opportunities: occupational matching, competition, fares and reliability are outside this model.
