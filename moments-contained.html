Independent BA dissertation · Durham University · 2025

Public art and representation in Rotterdam

I examined how people encountered and interpreted Moments Contained, Thomas J. Price’s statue of an anonymous Black woman beside Rotterdam Central. Through interviews, repeated site observations and analysis of institutional and media narratives, I investigated how a work celebrated as a symbol of inclusion was understood in everyday encounters.

My contribution
Research design, bilingual interviewing, site observation, discourse analysis and qualitative synthesis.

12 interviews · Dutch and English · Repeated field visits

The setting

Encounters beside Rotterdam Central

The open plaza and nearby station shaped the setting in which people encountered the statue. Across visits at different times of day, I observed more passing through than lingering. Its prominent location made the work available to many viewers, but did not guarantee sustained attention.

The study found a tension between visible representation, public approval and deeper engagement. Ambiguity allowed different readings, while also helping the sculpture fit comfortably within a civic story of inclusion.

Comparing narratives, interviews and observations

I compared what was said about the work, what participants made of it and what happened around it. These sources illuminate different aspects of the encounter; they are not interchangeable measures of inclusion.

Evidence and interpretation

Each view distinguishes what the sources show from the argument I developed.

How institutions and media framed the sculpture

The evidenceMuch of the institutional and media material described the sculpture through diversity, calmness, relatability and universal appeal. Other commentary questioned its anonymity or challenged its place in monumental art.

My interpretationI argued that framing narrows an apparently open artwork into recognisable stories. Its ambiguity can invite interpretation while also making it easier to celebrate inclusion without specifying what that inclusion entails.

This is an interpretation of published narratives, not evidence of a coordinated institutional intention.

Positive responses and uncertainty

The evidenceParticipants often responded positively, yet some struggled to explain the work's meaning or said they had not thought much about it. Appreciation and uncertainty appeared together rather than forming a simple positive-or-negative verdict.

My interpretationI treated hesitation as part of the evidence. The interviews suggested that feeling positively about a symbol does not necessarily produce a shared understanding of whom it represents or what it asks of the public.

Twelve unrecorded interviews from a convenience sample. These accounts are paraphrased; they are not survey percentages or representative public opinion.

Attention in a busy transit plaza

The evidenceFieldnotes described brief glances and pauses within a space dominated by movement. Some participants only looked more closely after being approached. The statue's stillness sat alongside the pace of the transit plaza.

My interpretationThis made spatial context part of the argument: attention depends on how a place is used as well as on the artwork itself. Passing by cannot, on its own, tell us whether a person felt indifferent, reassured or represented.

Qualitative field observations, not measured pedestrian counts or a causal test of plaza design.

Representation and the meaning of inclusion

My central argument was that symbolic representation can be meaningful and still leave the terms of recognition unresolved. The sculpture's openness permits different responses, but its calm, familiar presence can also be absorbed into an image of an already inclusive city. The case therefore asks how representation is framed, encountered and made acceptable, alongside who becomes visible.

Research design and interpretation

I developed a site-specific research question, conducted interviews and field observations, and compared participants’ accounts with public narratives. I treated each source according to what it could establish and reflected on how my own position shaped the research.

The contribution is an interpretive account of this place, with wider questions to explore. It is not a measure of the sculpture’s impact on social inclusion.

Study details

Fieldwork, analysis
and limitations

Fieldwork and analysis +

I conducted twelve interviews of 10-30 minutes in Dutch or English during field visits in December 2023 and January 2024. Participants were recruited near the statue through convenience sampling. I combined their accounts with morning, lunchtime and evening observations, fieldnotes and analysis of institutional and media coverage.

Whose perspective is represented? +

This is an interpretive account of one site. Most interviewees were white, interviews were not recorded, and my own position shaped both the conversations and their interpretation. The research does not establish how all publics experience the work or measure whether it changes social inclusion. Its stronger conclusions concern tensions in this case, with wider arguments presented as interpretations to explore further.
