Research note · Global Urbanism · Durham

Informality and the right to the city

I brought theories of informality into dialogue with the right to the city, using published cases to examine a tension: informal practices can be a means of exclusion and a way for residents to claim a place in urban life.

Comparative research / Stakeholder perspectives / Policy reasoning

How rules can produce informality

Roy’s account of informality challenged the assumption that informal activity simply exists outside governance. I applied that argument to published accounts from Delhi and Los Angeles, examining how selective enforcement and uncertain recognition shape who can remain in urban space. The comparison made regulation part of the explanation, rather than treating its absence as the problem.

Making room for agency and exploitation

I then connected housing occupations and street vending with Lefebvre’s right to the city and Holston’s insurgent citizenship: claims to shape urban life made through everyday action. Counterexamples mattered. Exploitative landlords and elite avoidance of rules showed why informality could not automatically be equated with justice.

From recognition to control

The resulting argument was that recognising informal practices must be connected to land, services and decision-making power. I used the distinction between participation offered by institutions and participation organised from below to question whether inclusion changes who controls resources. This kept the essay’s conclusion tied to material conditions, rather than recognition alone.

Scope of the work

A comparative interpretation of published urban studies. The contribution is the argument connecting governance, informal agency and redistribution, rather than an evaluation of a planning intervention.

Source & presentation +

Global Urbanism essay on informality and the right to citylife, Durham University. The comparison connects work by Ananya Roy, Henri Lefebvre, James Holston and Faranak Miraftab with published urban cases. The international examples are secondary research, not field visits.
