Research note · Geographies of Life and Living · Durham

Offshore borders and institutional responsibility

I used theories of power and spatial separation to compare published accounts of Mediterranean and Australian offshore border practices. The question was how control can extend across several places while responsibility becomes harder to locate.

Institutional comparison / Conceptual analysis / Research writing

Following an enforcement archipelago

The idea of an enforcement archipelago treats islands, detention facilities and organisations as a connected system. I used it to bring Mediterranean island and Libya-related practices into comparison with Australian offshore detention. It made the relationships between sites central to the analysis, rather than treating each facility as an isolated location.

What distance does to responsibility

I connected this spatial account with necropolitics, a framework concerned with how power distributes exposure to death and harm. My argument was that geographical separation and outsourced enforcement can distance people from protection while fragmenting accountability. Reading the two cases together helped trace how decisions, their implementation and their consequences could be separated across institutions and places.

Scope of the work

A critical interpretation of published research, completed in 2024. The comparison develops an argument about governance and responsibility; it does not determine the legal liability of particular actors.

Source & presentation +

Abandonment at Sea: The Necropolitics of Maritime Borders and Refugees, Durham University. The work connects literature on necropolitics, enforcement archipelagos and spaces of exception. Its historical cases are not presented as a summary of current border rules.
