Research note · Humanitarianism and the Human · Durham

Humanitarian response and institutional capacity

I brought public-health accounts of Yemen’s cholera response together with literature on humanitarian infrastructure and governance. The synthesis examined how damaged services, access restrictions and fragmented authority could reinforce one another.

Systems analysis / Evidence synthesis / Operational context

Connecting physical and institutional constraints

I used the concept of humanitarian infrastructure to examine more than the availability of supplies. Water systems, health facilities and transport sit alongside the permissions and organisational relationships needed to reach people. Organising the sources around these connections helped explain why a delivery problem could also be a problem of access and authority.

Whose needs shape a response?

The essay connected operational barriers with wider arguments about donor priorities, political visibility and humanitarian neutrality. Bringing these strands together made it possible to ask how the distribution of attention and resources relates to the needs described in the health literature.

From immediate relief to sustained capacity

My conclusion linked urgent assistance to infrastructure repair, local capacity and collaboration with local actors. The distinction matters because restoring a service and sustaining the conditions for it to function are different tasks. The argument emerged from comparing the scales and time horizons of the sources.

Scope of the work

A literature-based explanation of interacting constraints in a historical case. The work synthesises health, humanitarian and governance sources; it does not estimate intervention effects.

Source & presentation +

Cholera and Conflict: Navigating Humanitarian Challenges in Yemen, Durham University. The essay connects health literature with work on humanitarian infrastructure, including Pascucci, and wider arguments about humanitarian priorities and governance.
