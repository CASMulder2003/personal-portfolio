Agent-based modelling · UCL · 2026

Taxation in a simulated economy

Comparing inequality under a simple tax-and-redistribution rule.

I extended two existing Sugarscape simulations, in which agents gather and consume sugar, to test a proportional tax with equal redistribution. The aim was to compare inequality while holding the other behavioural rules fixed.

I tested eight tax rates over repeated runs and analysed inequality over time and across the resource distribution. The explorer below uses the saved results for Sugarscape 3.

Tax-rate comparison

How does inequality
change with the tax rate?

Agents harvest sugar, consume it and move through a spatial environment. I added a rule that taxes a proportion of each agent’s sugar every tick, then redistributes the total equally.

Eight tax rates were each tested over 20 repetitions. The comparison below combines the level of inequality with its time path and a Lorenz curve, which shows how sugar is distributed across the population.

Study details

Experiment design
and limitations

What I added to the models +

I introduced the tax-and-redistribute rule into Sugarscape 2: Constant Growback and Sugarscape 3: Wealth Distribution. The underlying movement, resource, metabolism and replacement rules remained those of the existing models.

I implemented or exported reporters for the Gini coefficient and richest 10% sugar share, then used BehaviourSpace to vary tax from 0 to 35% in five-percentage-point increments, with 20 repetitions and 1,000 ticks per configuration. For S3, I also exported cumulative wealth shares for Lorenz-curve comparisons.

The charts on this page cover S3 only. They show the archived experiment within its original parameter range.

Why compare levels, dynamics and distributions? +

A Gini coefficient provides a compact comparison but cannot show how quickly a system changes or which parts of a distribution differ. I paired it with time paths, top-share measures and cumulative distribution curves.

Repeated runs separate stochastic variation from the average response. The error bars here measure variation in run-level averages, rather than treating every tick as an independent experiment.

What this does not establish +

Sugar is an abstract resource. A tick is not a year, and a tax rate applied every tick is not comparable to an annual wealth tax. The models do not represent an empirically calibrated economy, tax avoidance or political and administrative constraints.

This is a controlled exploration of redistribution within a synthetic system. It does not identify an optimal real-world tax rate or demonstrate that the same effects would occur in households or markets.

Saved outputs, checks and values +

The explorer aggregates the original S3 export: 160 complete runs with 1,001 recorded steps each. The reconstructed Gini and top-share means match the archived cleaned CSVs within numerical precision. Population counts and the bounds and ordering of the distribution measures were checked.

No simulation was rerun. These checks establish faithful reconstruction of the archive, not external validation of the model. Lorenz points are means across runs and ticks 200–1000.

Source: CASA0011 Agent-based Modelling for Spatial Science, UCL, 2026. Based on the existing Sugarscape models, extended for this coursework.
